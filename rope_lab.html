Derivation

1. The model: Newton and Hooke only

Mass i sits at \mathbf r_i=(x_i,y_i). The two springs attached to it pull along themselves:

m\,\ddot{\mathbf r}_i \;=\; \mathbf F_i^{+} + \mathbf F_i^{-}, \qquad i = 1,\dots,N-1
\mathbf F_i^{+} \;=\; k\,\bigl(\ell_i-\ell_0\bigr)\,\frac{\mathbf r_{i+1}-\mathbf r_i}{\ell_i}, \qquad \ell_i \;=\; \bigl\lVert \mathbf r_{i+1}-\mathbf r_i \bigr\rVert

and \mathbf F_i^{-} likewise towards mass i − 1. The ends are fixed: \mathbf r_0=(0,0), \mathbf r_N=(L,0).

2. Equilibrium and tension

At rest the masses are evenly spaced and every spring has the same stretch. That stretch is the tension:

\mathbf r_i^{0} = (i\,s,\;0), \qquad s=\frac{L}{N}, \qquad T = k\,\bigl(s-\ell_0\bigr)

For T = 20 N the rest length is \ell_0 = s - T/k = 0.05 - 0.01 = 0.04\ \text{m}.

3. Small displacements

Let each mass move a little along the rope (u_i) and sideways (y_i): \mathbf r_i = (i s + u_i,\; y_i). Expanding the segment length:

\ell_i = \sqrt{\bigl(s+u_{i+1}-u_i\bigr)^2 + \bigl(y_{i+1}-y_i\bigr)^2} \;\approx\; s + \bigl(u_{i+1}-u_i\bigr) + \frac{\bigl(y_{i+1}-y_i\bigr)^2}{2s}

The sideways change enters only squared, so to first order the tension stays T. The vertical part of the right-hand spring force is therefore

F^{+}_{i,y} \;=\; k\,\bigl(\ell_i-\ell_0\bigr)\,\frac{y_{i+1}-y_i}{\ell_i} \;\approx\; T\,\frac{y_{i+1}-y_i}{s}

Adding the left spring gives two separate equations, one sideways and one along the rope:

m\,\ddot y_i \;=\; \frac{T}{s}\,\bigl(y_{i+1} - 2y_i + y_{i-1}\bigr)
m\,\ddot u_i \;=\; k\,\bigl(u_{i+1} - 2u_i + u_{i-1}\bigr)

The sideways stiffness is T/s. With no pretension it is zero, which is why a slack rope has no harmonics.

4. Matrix form

\mathbf M\,\ddot{\mathbf y} + \mathbf K\,\mathbf y = \mathbf 0, \qquad \mathbf M = m\,\mathbf I, \qquad \mathbf K = \frac{T}{s}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 & & & \\ -1 & 2 & -1 & & \\ & \ddots & \ddots & \ddots & \\ & & -1 & 2 & -1 \\ & & & -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}_{(N-1)\times(N-1)}

5. What a mode is

A normal mode is a motion where every mass keeps the same shape \boldsymbol\varphi and only its size changes in time: \mathbf y(t)=\boldsymbol\varphi\,q(t). Substituting gives m\,\ddot q\,\boldsymbol\varphi = -q\,\mathbf K\boldsymbol\varphi, which works only if

\mathbf K\,\boldsymbol\varphi = \omega^2\,\mathbf M\,\boldsymbol\varphi \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \ddot q = -\omega^2 q

This is analysis of the linearised equations, used only as a prediction. The simulation itself keeps integrating the full nonlinear forces.

6. Solving the eigenproblem exactly

Row i of the eigenproblem reads

\frac{T}{s}\,\bigl(-\varphi_{i-1} + 2\varphi_i - \varphi_{i+1}\bigr) = m\,\omega^2\,\varphi_i

Try \varphi_i = \sin(i\theta). It gives \varphi_0 = 0 automatically. The fixed right end needs \varphi_N = \sin(N\theta) = 0, so

\theta_n = \frac{n\pi}{N}, \qquad n = 1,\dots,N-1

With the identity

\sin\bigl((i+1)\theta\bigr) + \sin\bigl((i-1)\theta\bigr) = 2\cos\theta\,\sin(i\theta)

every row reduces to the same scalar equation:

\frac{T}{s}\,\bigl(2 - 2\cos\theta_n\bigr) = m\,\omega_n^2 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \omega_n^2 = \frac{2T}{m s}\bigl(1-\cos\theta_n\bigr) = \frac{4T}{m s}\,\sin^2\!\frac{n\pi}{2N}
f_n = \frac{\omega_n}{2\pi} = \frac{1}{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{T}{m\,s}}\;\sin\frac{n\pi}{2N}, \qquad \varphi^{(n)}_i = \sin\frac{i\,n\pi}{N}

7. The continuous string as a limit

With mass per length \mu = m/s, L = N s and wave speed c=\sqrt{T/\mu}, for n \ll N use \sin x \approx x:

f_n \;\approx\; \frac{1}{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{T}{m s}}\cdot\frac{n\pi}{2N} \;=\; \frac{n}{2L}\sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}} \;=\; \frac{n\,c}{2L}

The school formula is the low-mode limit. The discrete rope always lies below it:

\frac{f_n}{n c/2L} = \frac{\sin x_n}{x_n} \;\approx\; 1 - \frac{x_n^2}{6}, \qquad x_n = \frac{n\pi}{2N}

For n = 10, x_{10} = 0.393 and the ratio is 0.974, i.e. 2.6% low. That matches the table.

8. Modes along the rope and the time step

The axial equation has the same matrix with k in place of T/s:

f^{\mathrm{ax}}_n = \frac{1}{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}\;\sin\frac{n\pi}{2N}, \qquad \omega_{\max} = 2\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}\,\sin\frac{(N-1)\pi}{2N} \;\approx\; 2\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}

This fastest axial mode sets the step limit used in the other tabs: \omega_{\max}\,\Delta t < 2 for symplectic Euler and Verlet.

9. Numbers for this rope

m = \frac{0.2\ \text{kg}}{39} = 5.128\ \text{g}, \qquad s = 0.05\ \text{m}, \qquad T = 20\ \text{N}, \qquad k = 2000\ \text{N/m}
\sqrt{\frac{T}{m s}} = \sqrt{\frac{20}{0.005128 \cdot 0.05}} = 279.3\ \text{s}^{-1}
f_1 = \frac{279.3}{\pi}\,\sin\frac{\pi}{80} = 88.90 \cdot 0.03926 = 3.490\ \text{Hz}
f_1^{\mathrm{ax}} = \frac{1}{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{2000}{0.005128}}\;\sin\frac{\pi}{80} = 198.8 \cdot 0.03926 = 7.80\ \text{Hz}, \qquad f^{\mathrm{ax}}_{39} = 198.6\ \text{Hz}

10. Check 1: eigenvalues of the simulation's own forces

No hand algebra here. The full force function \mathbf F(\mathbf q) of the simulation, with all 2(N − 1) = 78 coordinates, is differentiated numerically at equilibrium:

J_{ab} = \frac{\partial F_a}{\partial q_b} \;\approx\; \frac{F_a\bigl(\mathbf q^0 + \varepsilon\,\mathbf e_b\bigr) - F_a\bigl(\mathbf q^0\bigr)}{\varepsilon}, \qquad \varepsilon = 10^{-7}\ \text{m}
\omega^2 \in \operatorname{eig}\!\Bigl(-\frac{1}{m}\,\mathbf J\Bigr)

This gives 78 frequencies: 39 sideways and 39 along the rope, interleaved (7.80 Hz axial lies between 6.975 and 10.449 Hz sideways). The sideways ones match the formula of step 6 to within 4\cdot 10^{-10} Hz.

11. Check 2: spectrum of a plucked simulated rope

A small Gaussian bump (2 mm high, 8 cm wide) is released from rest and integrated with Verlet, \Delta t = 0.2/\omega_{\max}, for 20 s with no damping and no drive. The height of mass 7 is recorded and transformed:

\hat Y(f) = \sum_{j=0}^{N_s-1} w_j\; y_7(t_j)\; e^{-2\pi i f t_j}, \qquad w_j = \sin^2\!\frac{\pi j}{N_s-1}

The window w_j (Hann) suppresses leakage from cutting the record. The resolution is \Delta f = 1/T_{\text{rec}} = 0.05\ \text{Hz}, and refining each peak with a parabola through its three highest bins puts it within 0.003 Hz of the formula (table above).

12. Where the linear theory stops

The term dropped in step 3 lengthens the rope. For a mode y = A\sin(n\pi x/L):

\Delta L \;\approx\; \frac{1}{2}\int_0^L \Bigl(\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\Bigr)^2 dx \;=\; \frac{(n\pi A)^2}{4L}

N springs in series act like one spring of stiffness k/N, so the tension and the frequency rise with amplitude:

\Delta T \;\approx\; \frac{k}{N}\,\Delta L, \qquad \frac{\Delta f}{f} \;\approx\; \frac{\Delta T}{2T}

For A = 5 cm in mode 1: ΔL = 3.1 mm, ΔT ≈ 0.15 N at full swing, so f rises by at most about 0.4%. The full simulation includes this automatically. The linear modes above do not.

1. Explicit Euler always adds energy

It moves the mass with the velocity from before the force acted. Going outward that velocity is too large, so it overshoots. Coming back it is again too large, so it overshoots again. In phase space both arrows start from the old point, so the step always leaves the circle outward: energy × (1 + ω²dt²) per step. A smaller dt only slows this down. That is why your rope blew up eventually even with damping.

2. Symplectic Euler and Verlet keep energy bounded

First the spring force changes v, then the mass moves with the new v. Moving outward the force has already braked it, so it goes slightly less far. Coming back it has already been sped up, so it arrives early. The errors have opposite signs in each half of the swing and cancel over a full period. For symplectic Euler the quantity y² + (v/ω)² − ω·dt·y·(v/ω) is conserved exactly, so the true energy can only wobble inside a fixed band. In matrix form: the step map has determinant exactly 1, so it never inflates or shrinks the set of states. Explicit Euler has determinant 1 + ω²dt². Velocity Verlet (half kick, drift, half kick) has the same property and is second order, at the same cost of one force evaluation per step. It is the standard choice in molecular dynamics and cloth simulation.

3. Runge–Kutta 4 is precise but leaks energy

RK4 samples the slope four times and averages them, so one step follows the true curve very closely (4th order). It is not symplectic, though: its determinant is 1 − (ω·dt)⁶/72 + …, slightly below 1. Energy drains a little every step, like invisible damping. With real damping in the rope this hardly matters, and RK4 even survives a larger step (ω·dt up to 2.83). For an undamped resonance it underestimates the amplitude, and it costs four force evaluations per step. For the same work, Verlet with four smaller steps keeps the energy better.

4. Choosing the step

The rope's fastest motion is the axial vibration of the stiff springs, ωmax = 2√(k/m). That frequency sets the step for every mass: symplectic Euler and Verlet need ωmax·dt < 2, RK4 needs < 2.83. The slider in the Rope tab is in these units, so the limits sit at 2.0 and 2.83.

5. Tension and damping are physics, not numerics

Without pretension a straight rope has no linear sideways restoring force. Stretch is about y²/(2·seg), so the force goes like y³. There is no wave speed and no harmonics to resonate with. Pretension gives c = √(T/μ) and harmonics fₙ = n·c/(2L). Gravity also provides tension, but uneven along the rope, so its modes are not simple multiples. Damping keeps a driven resonance bounded, as in a real rope.
