Sciama's Origin of Inertia
D. W. Sciama · Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society 113, 34 (1953)

Why is it hard to push things? Because the universe is there.

A modern, plain-language walkthrough of Sciama's “On the Origin of Inertia”, the first quantitative model of Mach's principle. Written in my own words, not a reprint of the paper.

§1The puzzle

Newton's second law, F = m·a, says acceleration is measured against something. Newton called that something absolute space. Ernst Mach objected: nobody can observe absolute space. What we can observe is that the frame where no inertial forces appear is the frame where the distant galaxies do not rotate. Mach suggested this is no coincidence. Inertia might be an interaction with all the matter in the universe.

Mach never gave a formula. In 1953 Dennis Sciama, then a young PhD student in Cambridge, built a deliberately simple model that does. His goal was not a complete theory of gravity, but a working example showing that Mach's idea can produce real numbers.

§2The key idea: gravity copied from electromagnetism

A charge at rest makes an electric field. A moving charge also makes a magnetic field, described by a vector potential A. And when a charge accelerates, its field picks up an extra piece that points against the acceleration and falls off only as 1/r, much more slowly than the static 1/r² field.

Sciama gave gravity the same structure. Masses have a scalar potential Φ, and moving masses also produce a gravitational vector potential:

Φ = −G ∫ ρ / r dV
A = Φ · v / c(1) ρ is mass density, v the velocity of the matter producing the field, c the speed of light.

The gravitational field felt by a body then has two parts, just like the electric field:

E = −∇Φ − (1/c) · ∂A/∂t(2) The first term is ordinary Newtonian gravity. The second appears only when the source matter changes its velocity.

§3Look at the universe from the moving body

Take a small test body. Around it are some nearby masses (the Earth, the Sun) and, far beyond, the smoothed-out universe of galaxies with average density ρ.

Now switch to the body's own rest frame. If the body moves with velocity v, then in its frame the whole universe streams past it with velocity −v. If the body accelerates, the entire universe accelerates the other way.

For a uniform universe the first term of (2) cancels: matter pulls equally from every side. Only the second term survives. Working it through with (1), the universe produces a field on the body of:

Euniverse = − (|Φ| / c²) · a(3) A field proportional to the body's acceleration and pointing against it. That is exactly what an inertial force looks like.

§4The postulate, and Newton's law falls out

Sciama's single assumption: in its own rest frame, the total gravitational field on any body is zero. A body never feels itself pulled, because it is always moving with the net field. Adding the local gravity g from nearby masses to the universe term (3):

g − (|Φ|/c²) · a = 0   ⇒   a = g · c² / |Φ|(4)

This is Newton's law of free fall, a = g, exactly when

|Φuniverse| = c²   or equivalently   G·M / (R·c²) ≈ 1(5) M and R are the mass and size of the observable universe. Multiply both sides of (4) by m and you get F = m·a, with the mass m now measuring how strongly the body couples to the distant universe.

§5Does the real universe satisfy this?

Here is the striking part. Galaxies beyond about R ≈ c/H (H is the Hubble constant) recede faster than light and cannot contribute, so Sciama cuts the integral off there. For a uniform sphere the potential at the centre is Φ = −2πGρR². With R = c/H:

|Φ| / c² = 2πGρ / H²(6)

Cosmology has its own natural density, the critical density ρc = 3H²/(8πG), and we write the actual density as Ω·ρc. Put that into (6) and the Hubble constant drops out completely:

|Φ| / c² = ¾ · Ω(7) Sciama's condition (5) becomes a statement about the density of the universe alone.

The black mark is the value Newton's law needs. With all of today's measured energy content (Ω ≈ 1) the model lands within a factor of about 1.3. Using ordinary matter only, it misses by a factor of about 25. Whether dark energy should count is a question the 1953 model cannot answer.

In 1953 the numbers were far less precise, and Sciama's point was the order of magnitude. Nobody had a reason to expect G, the density of the universe and the Hubble rate to combine into a number near 1. In his model it is not a coincidence but a necessity: G is set by the universe.

G ≈ c² / (2πρR²)   or   c² / G ≈ Σ mi / ri(8) The second form, summing over all masses mi at distances ri, is the version Alexander Unzicker uses in his own papers.

§6Where does your inertia come from?

Each shell of the universe at distance r contains mass proportional to r², while its potential falls off only as 1/r. So farther shells contribute more. The share of inertia coming from everything within distance r is (r/R)².

Uniform-density estimate with R = c/H ≈ 14 billion light years. The real Galaxy is denser than average, but even then the Sun adds about 10⁻⁸ and the Milky Way about 10⁻⁶ to Earth's |Φ|/c². Practically all inertia comes from the most distant galaxies.

§7Rotation and Newton's bucket

The same reasoning works for rotation. In the frame of a spinning bucket the universe rotates around it. Rotating matter is a mass current, which produces the gravitational analogue of a magnetic field. Sciama showed that this field gives the water exactly the centrifugal and Coriolis forces, with the same factor |Φ|/c² = 1 as before. The curved water surface is caused by the rotating galaxies, as Mach suggested, rather than by absolute space.

§8What follows from it

  1. Inertial and gravitational mass are equal. Both measure the same coupling, one to nearby matter and one to the whole universe. In Newton's theory this equality is an unexplained coincidence.
  2. An empty universe has no inertia. Remove the distant galaxies and |Φ| goes to zero, so equation (4) loses its meaning.
  3. G may change over cosmic time. If the universe expands and ρR² changes, G must change with it to keep (5) true. This connects to ideas of Dirac and later Brans and Dicke.
  4. Nearby matter should matter a tiny bit. If c²/G is a sum over masses, the varying Earth–Sun distance should change G by a part in a billion over the year. Unzicker and Fabian looked for this in superconducting-gravimeter data in 2006 and found no significant signal.

§9Limits of the model

Sciama presented this as a simplified model, not a replacement for general relativity. It is a linear theory, copying electromagnetism, while real gravity is non-linear. The cutoff at c/H and the instantaneous treatment of distant matter are rough. And the agreement in (7) depends on what you count as “the universe”. Sciama himself later worked mainly within general relativity, where Mach's principle is only partly realized, for example through frame dragging, which Gravity Probe B measured around the Earth.

The paper remains the clearest example of the Machian idea worked out with numbers: inertia appears as the field of the whole universe acting back on an accelerating body.
