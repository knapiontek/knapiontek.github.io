Differential geometry

Polar Coordinates in Euclidean Space

Basis vectors that change from point to point, and the Christoffel symbols that track the change. This is the first step towards Riemannian geometry.

Derivative of a vector field

Write a vector in components on a coordinate basis (summation over repeated indices):

\vec V = V^a\,\mathbf e_a

When the basis vectors depend on position, the product rule gives two terms:

\frac{\partial\vec V}{\partial x^b} = \frac{\partial V^a}{\partial x^b}\,\mathbf e_a + V^a\,\frac{\partial \mathbf e_a}{\partial x^b}

Polar basis vectors

\begin{cases} x = r\cos\phi\\ y = r\sin\phi \end{cases}

A coordinate basis vector is the derivative of the position along that coordinate. With Cartesian x^{b'}\in\{x,y\} and polar x^a\in\{r,\phi\}:

\mathbf e_a = \frac{\partial x^{b'}}{\partial x^{a}}\,\mathbf e_{b'} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf e_r \\ \mathbf e_\phi\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{\partial x}{\partial r} & \dfrac{\partial y}{\partial r} \\[8pt] \dfrac{\partial x}{\partial\phi} & \dfrac{\partial y}{\partial\phi} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf e_x \\ \mathbf e_y\end{bmatrix}
\begin{cases} \mathbf e_r = \cos\phi\,\mathbf e_x + \sin\phi\,\mathbf e_y \\ \mathbf e_\phi = -r\sin\phi\,\mathbf e_x + r\cos\phi\,\mathbf e_y \end{cases}

Note that \mathbf e_\phi is not a unit vector: its length is r. The metric is therefore

g_{ab} = \mathbf e_a\cdot\mathbf e_b = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ 0 & r^2\end{pmatrix}, \qquad ds^2 = dr^2 + r^2\,d\phi^2 .

How the basis changes

\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial \mathbf e_r}{\partial r} &= 0 \\[4pt] \frac{\partial \mathbf e_r}{\partial\phi} &= -\sin\phi\,\mathbf e_x + \cos\phi\,\mathbf e_y = \frac{1}{r}\,\mathbf e_\phi \\[4pt] \frac{\partial \mathbf e_\phi}{\partial r} &= -\sin\phi\,\mathbf e_x + \cos\phi\,\mathbf e_y = \frac{1}{r}\,\mathbf e_\phi \\[4pt] \frac{\partial \mathbf e_\phi}{\partial\phi} &= -r\cos\phi\,\mathbf e_x - r\sin\phi\,\mathbf e_y = -r\,\mathbf e_r \end{aligned}

Christoffel symbols

Every derivative of a basis vector is again a combination of basis vectors. The coefficients are the Christoffel symbols:

\frac{\partial \mathbf e_a}{\partial x^b} = \Gamma^c_{ab}\,\mathbf e_c \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \Gamma^\phi_{r\phi} = \Gamma^\phi_{\phi r} = \frac1r,\qquad \Gamma^r_{\phi\phi} = -r,

All other symbols are zero. Substituting into the product rule gives the covariant derivative:

\frac{\partial\vec V}{\partial x^b} = \left(\frac{\partial V^c}{\partial x^b} + V^a\,\Gamma^c_{ab}\right)\mathbf e_c \;\equiv\; \nabla_b V^c\,\mathbf e_c

As a check, the divergence \nabla_a V^a = \partial_r V^r + \partial_\phi V^\phi + \tfrac1r V^r is the familiar polar formula. The same symbols follow from the metric alone:

\Gamma^c_{ab} = \tfrac12\, g^{cd}\left(\partial_a g_{db} + \partial_b g_{da} - \partial_d g_{ab}\right).

That formula is what carries the idea over to curved spaces, where there is no Cartesian frame to start from.
