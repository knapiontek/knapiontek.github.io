Vector Calculus in Cartesian Coordinates

The gradient, curl and divergence defined from first principles as limits of sums and integrals, the integral theorems that follow from those definitions, and worked examples.

1Notation

Throughout, \(U(x,y,z)\) is a scalar field and \(\E(x,y,z) = E_x\ii + E_y\jj + E_z\kk\) is a vector field, both assumed smooth (continuously differentiable as many times as needed). The unit vectors along the axes are \(\ii, \jj, \kk\), and the position vector is \(\r = x\ii + y\jj + z\kk\).

All three differential operators are built from the formal vector

\[ \grad = \ii\,\pd{}{x} + \jj\,\pd{}{y} + \kk\,\pd{}{z}, \]

which produces the gradient \(\grad U\) (a vector), the divergence \(\grad\cdot\E\) (a scalar) and the curl \(\grad\times\E\) (a vector). Below each one is first defined geometrically, without reference to coordinates, and the Cartesian formula is then derived from that definition.

Infinitesimal elements: \(\dl\) is a vector line element tangent to a curve, \(\dS = \n\,\d S\) is a vector area element whose direction \(\n\) is the unit normal to the surface, and \(\dV = \d x\,\d y\,\d z\) is a volume element.

For two arbitrary points \(A\) and \(B\), the difference of the scalar field between them is

\[ \Delta U = U(B) - U(A). \]

2Line, surface and volume integrals

Each integral is the limit of a sum over small pieces of the domain.

Line integral

Split a curve \(l\) into short segments \(\Delta\mathbf{l}_i\), each a small displacement vector along the curve, and add up the component of \(\E\) along each segment:

\[ \int_l \E\cdot\dl = \lim_{|\Delta\mathbf{l}_i|\to 0}\ \sum_i \E(\r_i)\cdot\Delta\mathbf{l}_i . \]

If \(\E\) is a force, this is the work done along the path. When the curve is closed it is written \(\oint_l\) and called the circulation of \(\E\) around \(l\).

Surface (flux) integral

Split a surface \(S\) into small patches \(\Delta\mathbf{S}_i = \n_i\,\Delta S_i\) and add up the normal component of \(\E\) through each:

\[ \int_S \E\cdot\dS = \lim_{\Delta S_i\to 0}\ \sum_i \E(\r_i)\cdot\n_i\,\Delta S_i . \]

This is the flux of \(\E\) through \(S\). For a closed surface \(\oint_S\), the normal is taken to point outward.

Volume integral

\[ \int_V U\,\dV = \lim_{\Delta V_i\to 0}\ \sum_i U(\r_i)\,\Delta V_i . \]
Orientation rule When a surface \(S\) has a boundary curve \(\partial S\), the curve is traversed counter-clockwise as seen from the side \(\n\) points to (right-hand rule: thumb along \(\n\), fingers along the curve).

3Gradient of a scalar field

Definition

Move a small distance \(\Delta l\) from a point in the direction of a unit vector \(\n\). The rate of change of \(U\) along that direction is the directional derivative

\[ D_{\n}U = \lim_{\Delta l\to 0}\frac{U(\r + \n\,\Delta l) - U(\r)}{\Delta l}. \]

The gradient \(\grad U\) is the one vector that gives this rate for every direction at once:

\[ D_{\n}U = \grad U\cdot\n \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \d U = \grad U\cdot\dl . \]

Cartesian components

Choosing \(\n = \ii\) moves only \(x\), so the \(x\)-component of \(\grad U\) is the ordinary partial derivative with respect to \(x\); likewise for \(y\) and \(z\):

\[ \begin{aligned} (\grad U)_x &= \lim_{\Delta x\to 0}\frac{U(x+\Delta x,y,z)-U(x,y,z)}{\Delta x} = \pd{U}{x},\\[4pt] (\grad U)_y &= \lim_{\Delta y\to 0}\frac{U(x,y+\Delta y,z)-U(x,y,z)}{\Delta y} = \pd{U}{y},\\[4pt] (\grad U)_z &= \lim_{\Delta z\to 0}\frac{U(x,y,z+\Delta z)-U(x,y,z)}{\Delta z} = \pd{U}{z}. \end{aligned} \]
Gradient \[ \grad U = \pd{U}{x}\,\ii + \pd{U}{y}\,\jj + \pd{U}{z}\,\kk = \begin{bmatrix}\pd{U}{x}\\[6pt] \pd{U}{y}\\[6pt] \pd{U}{z}\end{bmatrix} \]

The three partial derivatives are components of a vector, not terms of a sum. Their sum \(\partial_x U + \partial_y U + \partial_z U\) is just the directional derivative along \((1,1,1)\) multiplied by \(\sqrt3\), which has no special meaning.

Geometric meaning

Since \(D_{\n}U = |\grad U|\cos\theta\), where \(\theta\) is the angle between \(\n\) and \(\grad U\):

  • \(\grad U\) points in the direction in which \(U\) increases fastest;
  • \(|\grad U|\) is that fastest rate of increase;
  • moving perpendicular to \(\grad U\) leaves \(U\) unchanged, so \(\grad U\) is normal to the level surfaces \(U = \text{const}\).

Line integral of a gradient

Along each short segment of a path from \(A\) to \(B\), \(\grad U\cdot\Delta\mathbf{l}_i\) is the small change \(\Delta U_i\) of \(U\) across that segment. The changes add up and all intermediate values cancel:

\[ \int_A^B \grad U\cdot\dl = \lim_{|\Delta\mathbf{l}_i|\to 0}\sum_i \grad U\cdot\Delta\mathbf{l}_i = \sum_i \Delta U_i = U(B) - U(A) = \Delta U . \]
Fundamental theorem for gradients \[ \int_A^B \grad U\cdot\dl = U(B) - U(A) \] The result depends only on the end points, not on the path between them.

Closed line integral of a gradient

For a closed path the end point coincides with the start, \(B = A\), so

\[ \oint_l \grad U\cdot\dl = U(A) - U(A) = 0 . \]

4Curl (rotation) of a vector field

Definition

Take a small flat surface \(\Delta S\) with unit normal \(\n\) and boundary loop \(\partial(\Delta S)\), oriented by the right-hand rule. The component of the curl along \(\n\) is the circulation per unit area:

\[ \n\cdot(\grad\times\E) = \lim_{\Delta S\to 0}\frac{1}{\Delta S}\oint_{\partial(\Delta S)}\E\cdot\dl . \]

Taking \(\n = \ii, \jj, \kk\) in turn gives the three components of the vector \(\grad\times\E\).

Cartesian components

For the \(z\)-component, take \(\n = \kk\) and a rectangle with sides \(\d x\), \(\d y\) in the \(xy\)-plane, traversed counter-clockwise as seen from above:

To first order, each edge contributes the tangential component of \(\E\) on that edge times its length. Edges 3 and 4 are traversed in the negative \(x\) and \(y\) directions:

\[ \oint \E\cdot\dl \approx E_x(x,y)\,\d x + E_y(x+\d x,y)\,\d y - E_x(x,y+\d y)\,\d x - E_y(x,y)\,\d y . \]

Group the two \(E_y\) terms (opposite vertical edges) and the two \(E_x\) terms (opposite horizontal edges), then divide by the area \(\d x\,\d y\):

\[ \begin{aligned} (\grad\times\E)_z &= \lim_{\d x,\d y\to 0}\frac{\big[E_y(x+\d x,y)-E_y(x,y)\big]\d y - \big[E_x(x,y+\d y)-E_x(x,y)\big]\d x}{\d x\,\d y}\\[6pt] &= \lim_{\d x\to 0}\frac{E_y(x+\d x,y)-E_y(x,y)}{\d x} - \lim_{\d y\to 0}\frac{E_x(x,y+\d y)-E_x(x,y)}{\d y}\\[6pt] &= \pd{E_y}{x} - \pd{E_x}{y}. \end{aligned} \]

The other two components follow by the cyclic substitution \(x\to y\to z\to x\):

Curl \[ \grad\times\E = \begin{bmatrix} \pd{E_z}{y} - \pd{E_y}{z}\\[8pt] \pd{E_x}{z} - \pd{E_z}{x}\\[8pt] \pd{E_y}{x} - \pd{E_x}{y} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{vmatrix} \ii & \jj & \kk\\[2pt] \partial_x & \partial_y & \partial_z\\[2pt] E_x & E_y & E_z \end{vmatrix} \]

Stokes' theorem

Cover a surface \(S\) with many small patches. Summing the circulation of every patch, each interior edge is traversed twice in opposite directions by its two neighbouring patches, so those contributions cancel. Only the outer boundary \(\partial S\) survives. On the other hand, by the definition of curl, the circulation of each small patch equals \((\grad\times\E)\cdot\n\,\Delta S\). Therefore

Stokes' theorem \[ \int_S(\grad\times\E)\cdot\dS = \oint_{\partial S}\E\cdot\dl \]

Curl of a gradient

Put \(\E = \grad U\) into the definition. The circulation of a gradient around any closed loop is zero (section 3), so every component of the curl vanishes:

\[ \n\cdot(\grad\times\grad U) = \lim_{\Delta S\to 0}\frac{1}{\Delta S}\oint_{\partial(\Delta S)}\grad U\cdot\dl = 0 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \grad\times\grad U = \mathbf{0}. \]

The same result in components: for instance \((\grad\times\grad U)_z = \dfrac{\partial^2 U}{\partial x\,\partial y} - \dfrac{\partial^2 U}{\partial y\,\partial x} = 0\), because mixed partial derivatives of a smooth function are equal.

Curl-free fields have a scalar potential

The converse also holds, provided the region has no holes that a loop could wrap around (it is simply connected). The chain of reasoning is:

\[ \begin{aligned} \grad\times\E = \mathbf{0} &\;\Longrightarrow\; \oint_l \E\cdot\dl = \int_S(\grad\times\E)\cdot\dS = 0 \text{ for every loop } l &&\text{(Stokes)}\\[4pt] &\;\Longrightarrow\; \int_A^B \E\cdot\dl \text{ is the same for every path from } A \text{ to } B\\[4pt] &\;\Longrightarrow\; U(P) = \int_{P_0}^{P}\E\cdot\dl \text{ is a well-defined function of } P\\[4pt] &\;\Longrightarrow\; \d U = \E\cdot\dl \;\Longrightarrow\; \E = \grad U . \end{aligned} \]

The second step uses the fact that two paths from \(A\) to \(B\) together form a closed loop (one of them reversed), whose circulation is zero. \(P_0\) is any fixed reference point; changing it adds a constant to \(U\).

Conservative fields On a simply connected region the following are equivalent: \(\;\E = \grad U\) for some \(U\); \(\;\oint\E\cdot\dl = 0\) for every loop; \(\;\int_A^B\E\cdot\dl\) is path-independent; \(\;\grad\times\E = \mathbf{0}\).
Caveat The field \(\E = \dfrac{-y\,\ii + x\,\jj}{x^2+y^2}\) has \(\grad\times\E = \mathbf{0}\) everywhere except on the \(z\)-axis, where it is undefined, yet its circulation around a circle about the axis is \(2\pi\). Without the simply-connected condition the last equivalence fails.

In physics the potential is usually defined with the opposite sign, \(\E = -\grad\varphi\), so that the field points from high to low potential. This changes nothing above.

Closed surface integral of a curl

A closed surface \(S\) (such as a sphere) has no boundary. Cut it along any closed curve \(l\) into two caps \(S_1\) and \(S_2\). With outward normals, the right-hand rule makes the two caps run around \(l\) in opposite directions, so by Stokes' theorem

\[ \oint_S(\grad\times\E)\cdot\dS = \int_{S_1}(\grad\times\E)\cdot\dS + \int_{S_2}(\grad\times\E)\cdot\dS = \oint_l\E\cdot\dl - \oint_l\E\cdot\dl = 0 . \]

5Divergence of a vector field

Definition

The divergence is the outward flux per unit volume through the surface of a small region:

\[ \grad\cdot\E = \lim_{\Delta V\to 0}\frac{1}{\Delta V}\oint_{\partial(\Delta V)}\E\cdot\dS . \]

Positive divergence marks a source (more field leaves than enters); negative divergence marks a sink.

Cartesian form

Take a small box with edges \(\d x, \d y, \d z\) and corner at \((x,y,z)\). Its six faces contribute:

FaceOutward normalFlux
at \(x\)\(-\ii\)\(-E_x(x,y,z)\,\d y\,\d z\)
at \(x+\d x\)\(+\ii\)\(+E_x(x+\d x,y,z)\,\d y\,\d z\)
at \(y\)\(-\jj\)\(-E_y(x,y,z)\,\d x\,\d z\)
at \(y+\d y\)\(+\jj\)\(+E_y(x,y+\d y,z)\,\d x\,\d z\)
at \(z\)\(-\kk\)\(-E_z(x,y,z)\,\d x\,\d y\)
at \(z+\d z\)\(+\kk\)\(+E_z(x,y,z+\d z)\,\d x\,\d y\)

Adding them and dividing by \(\d V = \d x\,\d y\,\d z\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \grad\cdot\E &= \lim_{\d V\to 0}\frac{\big[E_x(x+\d x)-E_x(x)\big]\d y\,\d z + \big[E_y(y+\d y)-E_y(y)\big]\d x\,\d z + \big[E_z(z+\d z)-E_z(z)\big]\d x\,\d y}{\d x\,\d y\,\d z}\\[6pt] &= \lim_{\d x\to 0}\frac{E_x(x+\d x)-E_x(x)}{\d x} + \lim_{\d y\to 0}\frac{E_y(y+\d y)-E_y(y)}{\d y} + \lim_{\d z\to 0}\frac{E_z(z+\d z)-E_z(z)}{\d z} \end{aligned} \]

(only the varying argument is written in each term).

Divergence \[ \grad\cdot\E = \pd{E_x}{x} + \pd{E_y}{y} + \pd{E_z}{z} \]

Divergence theorem (Gauss)

Fill a volume \(V\) with many small boxes. Summing the outward flux of every box, each interior face is shared by two neighbours whose outward normals are opposite, so those contributions cancel and only the outer surface \(\partial V\) remains. By the definition of divergence, the flux out of each small box equals \((\grad\cdot\E)\,\Delta V_i\):

\[ \int_V(\grad\cdot\E)\,\dV = \lim_{\Delta V_i\to 0}\sum_i \frac{\oint_{\partial(\Delta V_i)}\E\cdot\dS}{\Delta V_i}\,\Delta V_i = \lim_{\Delta V_i\to 0}\sum_i \oint_{\partial(\Delta V_i)}\E\cdot\dS = \oint_{\partial V}\E\cdot\dS . \]
Divergence theorem \[ \int_V(\grad\cdot\E)\,\dV = \oint_{\partial V}\E\cdot\dS \]

Divergence of a curl

Apply the divergence theorem to \(\grad\times\F\) and use the result of section 4 that the flux of a curl through any closed surface is zero:

\[ \int_V\grad\cdot(\grad\times\F)\,\dV = \oint_{\partial V}(\grad\times\F)\cdot\dS = 0 . \]

This holds for every volume \(V\), however small, so the integrand itself is zero:

\[ \grad\cdot(\grad\times\F) = 0 . \]

In components the six second derivatives cancel in pairs, again by equality of mixed partials: \(\partial_x(\partial_y F_z - \partial_z F_y) + \partial_y(\partial_z F_x - \partial_x F_z) + \partial_z(\partial_x F_y - \partial_y F_x) = 0\).

Divergence-free fields have a vector potential

Conversely, a field with zero divergence is the curl of some other field, provided the region is contractible (for example, all of space, or any ball or box):

\[ \grad\cdot\E = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \E = \grad\times\F \text{ for some } \F . \]

Such an \(\E\) is called solenoidal, and \(\F\) a vector potential. It is not unique: since \(\grad\times\grad\chi = \mathbf{0}\), the field \(\F + \grad\chi\) works equally well for any scalar \(\chi\).

6Second derivatives and identities

Combining the operators in pairs gives five second-order expressions. Two always vanish (shown above):

\[ \grad\times(\grad U) = \mathbf{0}, \qquad \grad\cdot(\grad\times\E) = 0 . \]

The divergence of a gradient is the Laplacian:

\[ \grad\cdot\grad U = \grad^2 U = \frac{\partial^2 U}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2 U}{\partial y^2} + \frac{\partial^2 U}{\partial z^2}. \]

Applied to each Cartesian component it gives the vector Laplacian \(\grad^2\E = \grad^2E_x\,\ii + \grad^2E_y\,\jj + \grad^2E_z\,\kk\), which appears in the curl of a curl:

\[ \grad\times(\grad\times\E) = \grad(\grad\cdot\E) - \grad^2\E . \]

Product rules

For scalar fields \(U, W\) and vector fields \(\E, \F\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \grad(UW) &= U\,\grad W + W\,\grad U\\[2pt] \grad\cdot(U\E) &= U\,\grad\cdot\E + \E\cdot\grad U\\[2pt] \grad\times(U\E) &= U\,\grad\times\E + \grad U\times\E\\[2pt] \grad\cdot(\E\times\F) &= \F\cdot(\grad\times\E) - \E\cdot(\grad\times\F) \end{aligned} \]

7Worked examples

Example 1: a rigid rotation

\[ \A = \begin{bmatrix} y\\ -x\\ 0\end{bmatrix} \]

At \((1,0,0)\) the field is \(-\jj\), at \((0,1,0)\) it is \(+\ii\): the vectors circle the \(z\)-axis clockwise when viewed from above, with speed growing in proportion to distance from the axis, like a spinning disc.

Curl.

\[ \grad\times\A = \begin{bmatrix} \pd{(0)}{y} - \pd{(-x)}{z}\\[8pt] \pd{(y)}{z} - \pd{(0)}{x}\\[8pt] \pd{(-x)}{x} - \pd{(y)}{y} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0\\ 0\\ -1-1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0\\ 0\\ -2\end{bmatrix}. \]

The curl is constant and points along \(-\kk\), matching the clockwise rotation (right-hand rule). For a rigid rotation with angular velocity \(\boldsymbol\omega\) the curl is always \(2\boldsymbol\omega\); here \(\boldsymbol\omega = -\kk\).

Check with Stokes' theorem. On a circle of radius \(R\) in the \(xy\)-plane, traversed counter-clockwise, \(\r = (R\cos t,\,R\sin t,\,0)\), \(\dl = (-R\sin t,\,R\cos t,\,0)\,\d t\) and \(\A = (R\sin t,\,-R\cos t,\,0)\):

\[ \oint\A\cdot\dl = \int_0^{2\pi}\big(-R^2\sin^2 t - R^2\cos^2 t\big)\,\d t = -2\pi R^2, \qquad \int_S(\grad\times\A)\cdot\kk\,\d S = (-2)\cdot\pi R^2 = -2\pi R^2. \;\checkmark \]

The circulation is not zero, so \(\A\) is not the gradient of any scalar field.

Divergence. \(\grad\cdot\A = \pd{(y)}{x} + \pd{(-x)}{y} + 0 = 0\). The field is solenoidal, and indeed \(\A = \grad\times\F\) with \(\F = \tfrac12(x^2+y^2)\,\kk\): for \(\F = f\,\kk\) the curl is \((\partial_y f,\,-\partial_x f,\,0) = (y,\,-x,\,0)\).

Example 2: a shear flow

\[ \A = \begin{bmatrix} y^2\\ x\\ 0\end{bmatrix} \]

Curl. Only the \(z\)-component can be non-zero, since nothing depends on \(z\) and \(A_z = 0\):

\[ \grad\times\A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 - 0\\[4pt] 0 - 0\\[4pt] \pd{(x)}{x} - \pd{(y^2)}{y} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0\\ 0\\ 1-2y\end{bmatrix}. \]

Unlike Example 1, the rotation varies from place to place: counter-clockwise below the line \(y = \tfrac12\), clockwise above it, and zero on it.

Check with Stokes' theorem on the rectangle \(0\le x\le1,\ 0\le y\le2\), counter-clockwise:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{bottom } (y=0,\ x: 0\to1):&\quad \int_0^1 A_x\,\d x = \int_0^1 0\,\d x = 0\\ \text{right } (x=1,\ y: 0\to2):&\quad \int_0^2 A_y\,\d y = \int_0^2 1\,\d y = 2\\ \text{top } (y=2,\ x: 1\to0):&\quad \int_1^0 A_x\,\d x = \int_1^0 4\,\d x = -4\\ \text{left } (x=0,\ y: 2\to0):&\quad \int_2^0 A_y\,\d y = \int_2^0 0\,\d y = 0 \end{aligned} \] \[ \oint\A\cdot\dl = -2, \qquad \int_0^1\!\!\int_0^2 (1-2y)\,\d y\,\d x = \big[y - y^2\big]_0^2 = -2. \;\checkmark \]

Divergence. \(\grad\cdot\A = \pd{(y^2)}{x} + \pd{(x)}{y} = 0\). A vector potential is \(\F = \big(\tfrac13y^3 - \tfrac12x^2\big)\,\kk\), since \(\partial_y f = y^2\) and \(-\partial_x f = x\).

Example 3: a conservative field

\[ \A = \begin{bmatrix} 2xy + z\\ x^2\\ x\end{bmatrix} \]

Curl.

\[ \grad\times\A = \begin{bmatrix} \pd{(x)}{y} - \pd{(x^2)}{z}\\[8pt] \pd{(2xy+z)}{z} - \pd{(x)}{x}\\[8pt] \pd{(x^2)}{x} - \pd{(2xy+z)}{y} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0-0\\ 1-1\\ 2x-2x\end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{0}. \]

All of space is simply connected, so \(\A = \grad U\) for some \(U\). Integrating \(A_x = \partial_x U\) gives \(U = x^2y + xz + g(y,z)\); then \(\partial_y U = x^2 + \partial_y g\) must equal \(x^2\) and \(\partial_z U = x + \partial_z g\) must equal \(x\), so \(g\) is a constant:

\[ U = x^2y + xz \quad(+\text{const}). \]

Line integral. From the origin to \((1,2,3)\), along any path:

\[ \int_{(0,0,0)}^{(1,2,3)}\A\cdot\dl = U(1,2,3) - U(0,0,0) = 2 + 3 = 5 . \]

Divergence. \(\grad\cdot\A = 2y + 0 + 0 = 2y\), which is also \(\grad^2 U\).

Example 4: the radial field

\[ \A = \r = \begin{bmatrix} x\\ y\\ z\end{bmatrix} \]

\(\grad\cdot\r = 1 + 1 + 1 = 3\), \(\;\grad\times\r = \mathbf{0}\), and \(\r = \grad\big(\tfrac12|\r|^2\big)\).

Check with the divergence theorem on a sphere of radius \(R\) centred at the origin. On the sphere \(\n = \r/R\), so \(\r\cdot\n = R\):

\[ \oint_S\r\cdot\dS = R\cdot4\pi R^2 = 4\pi R^3, \qquad \int_V 3\,\dV = 3\cdot\tfrac43\pi R^3 = 4\pi R^3. \;\checkmark \]

8Summary

OperatorGeometric definitionCartesian formIntegral theorem
Gradient \(\grad U\) \(\d U = \grad U\cdot\dl\) \((\partial_x U,\ \partial_y U,\ \partial_z U)\) \(\int_A^B\grad U\cdot\dl = U(B)-U(A)\)
Curl \(\grad\times\E\) circulation per unit area \((\partial_y E_z-\partial_z E_y,\ \partial_z E_x-\partial_x E_z,\ \partial_x E_y-\partial_y E_x)\) \(\int_S(\grad\times\E)\cdot\dS = \oint_{\partial S}\E\cdot\dl\)
Divergence \(\grad\cdot\E\) outward flux per unit volume \(\partial_x E_x+\partial_y E_y+\partial_z E_z\) \(\int_V\grad\cdot\E\,\dV = \oint_{\partial V}\E\cdot\dS\)

Each theorem has the same shape: integrating a derivative over a region equals evaluating the original field on the region's boundary. And each "derivative of a derivative" that vanishes is the statement that a boundary has no boundary:

\[ \grad\times\grad U = \mathbf{0} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{closed loops have no end points;} \qquad \grad\cdot(\grad\times\E) = 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{closed surfaces have no boundary curve.} \]
